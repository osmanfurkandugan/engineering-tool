HOW IT WORKS

What Is Voltage Drop, and Why Does It Matter?

Voltage drop is the loss of voltage between a power source and a load, caused by the resistance (and, to a smaller extent, reactance) of the cable conductors carrying the current. Every cable has some resistance, so some voltage is inevitably lost as heat along its length — the question is whether that loss stays within an acceptable limit.

Excessive voltage drop causes equipment to receive less voltage than it needs: motors run hotter and less efficiently, lighting dims, and sensitive electronics can misbehave or fail to start. That's why most wiring regulations set a maximum acceptable voltage drop — commonly 3% to 5% of the nominal voltage, depending on the standard and circuit type.

What Affects Voltage Drop?

Four factors drive voltage drop: the current flowing through the cable (higher current means more drop), the cable length (longer cables have more resistance), the cross-sectional area (thicker conductors have less resistance), and the conductor material (copper conducts better than aluminum of the same size). Operating temperature also matters — resistance rises as conductors heat up.

DC vs. Single-Phase vs. Three-Phase

In DC and single-phase AC circuits, current flows out to the load through one conductor and back through another, so the effective cable length used in the calculation is double the one-way distance. In three-phase systems, the return path is provided by the other two phases, so only the one-way length is used — but the drop is calculated with a √3 factor to account for the three-phase relationship between line and phase voltages.

DC: ΔU = 2 × I × R × L
1-phase AC: ΔU = 2 × I × L × (R·cosφ + X·sinφ)
3-phase AC: ΔU = √3 × I × L × (R·cosφ + X·sinφ)

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an acceptable voltage drop percentage?

Most wiring regulations recommend keeping total voltage drop within 3% to 5% of the nominal supply voltage, though the exact limit depends on the applicable standard and installation type.

Does cable length mean one-way or round-trip distance?

Always enter the one-way distance from source to load. This calculator automatically accounts for the return path for DC and single-phase circuits, and applies the correct three-phase factor for three-phase circuits.

Why is aluminum cable's voltage drop higher than copper?

Aluminum has lower conductivity than copper, so for the same cross-sectional area, an aluminum conductor has more resistance — resulting in a larger voltage drop for the same current and length.
